# T1 — Identity, scope and limits of one vector

**Source checked:** *ASI Intern Recruitment 2026*, T1, PDF pp. 8–11. The relation and near-miss definitions used below are on PDF pp. 4–5. The answer-sheet entries are collected at the end. No experimental results are claimed.

## A.1 — Relations between questions

Let (A(q)) be the set of complete answers to question (q). As in the paper, assume it is upward closed under entailment: if a statement entails a complete answer to (q), the statement is itself a complete answer to (q). The paper defines (q_1\succeq q_2) (“(q_1) is broader”) to mean that every complete answer to (q_1) yields a complete answer to (q_2).

### (i) Answer-set characterization

Suppose (q_1\succeq q_2). Take any (a\in A(q_1)). By subsumption, (a) entails some complete answer (b\in A(q_2)). Since (A(q_2)) is upward closed under entailment, (a\in A(q_2)). This holds for every (a\in A(q_1)), so

\[
A(q_1)\subseteq A(q_2).
\]

Conversely, if (A(q_1)\subseteq A(q_2)), every complete answer to (q_1) is already a complete answer to (q_2), and therefore yields one. Thus

\[
\boxed{q_1\succeq q_2\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A(q_1)\subseteq A(q_2).}
\]

The direction of inclusion is important: the broader question has the smaller set of answers that fully resolve it. A result that completely resolves a broad question must also resolve the narrower one under this definition.

### (ii) Equivalence, preorder, and symmetric part

By the paper’s definition, (q_1\equiv q_2) exactly when a complete answer to either is a complete answer to the other. In set terms,

\[
q_1\equiv q_2\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A(q_1)=A(q_2).
\]

Equality is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, so \(equiv\) is an equivalence relation. From (i), \(succeq\) is set inclusion in reverse question-specificity order. It is reflexive because (A(q)\subseteq A(q)), and transitive because set inclusion is transitive. Therefore \(succeq\) is a preorder. Its symmetric part is

\[
q_1\succeq q_2\ \text{and}\ q_2\succeq q_1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
A(q_1)\subseteq A(q_2)\ \text{and}\ A(q_2)\subseteq A(q_1)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q_1\equiv q_2.
\]

It is not necessarily antisymmetric on question strings: differently worded questions may have the same complete-answer set. The quotient by \(equiv\), however, is partially ordered by subsumption.

### (iii) Strictly broader realistic pairs

For both examples, assume the protocols and answer format are fixed. A complete answer to the broad question is a table covering every listed method; the narrow question asks for just one row. A complete table therefore answers the narrow question, but the narrow answer does not determine the other rows. The answer sets are strictly included.

1. \(q_1\): “How does each of random cropping, colour jitter, MixUp, and CutMix affect ImageNet top-1 accuracy for ResNet-50 under the fixed training protocol?”
   \(q_2\): “How does colour jitter affect ImageNet top-1 accuracy for ResNet-50 under that protocol?”  Thus (q_1\succeq q_2), but (q_2\not\succeq q_1).
2. \(q_1\): “What are the latency and validation-accuracy results for each of SGD, AdamW, and Adafactor on the fixed Transformer benchmark?”
   \(q_2\): “What are the latency and validation-accuracy results for AdamW on that benchmark?”  Again (q_1\succeq q_2), but (q_2\not\succeq q_1).

These examples depend on the specified exhaustive answer format. Without that assumption, ordinary-language “broader” can be ambiguous, which is why the answer-set definition matters.

### (iv) Existential “Can X improve Y?” questions

Let \(q_1\) ask whether any data augmentation can improve contrastive pre-training, and \(q_2\) ask whether colour jitter can. A complete positive answer to (q_1) could give random cropping as a successful example; it would establish that some augmentation works but would say nothing about colour jitter. Thus a single positive witness to the broad existential question does not, in general, completely answer the narrower one. A positive colour-jitter example happens to answer both, but that special witness does not make every complete answer to (q_1) an answer to (q_2). The intuition “the broader question subsumes the narrower one” fails when the broad question is existential and can be satisfied by a different instance.

## A.2 — Threshold similarity is not transitive

Let \(\theta=\arccos(0.8)\), so \(\cos\theta=0.8\). Choose unit vectors at angles (0,\theta,2\theta):

\[
u=(1,0),\qquad v=(0.8,0.6),\qquad w=(\cos 2\theta,\sin 2\theta)=(0.28,0.96).
\]

They are unit length because (0.8^2+0.6^2=1) and (0.28^2+0.96^2=1). Their cosines are

\[
\cos(u,v)=u\cdot v=0.8,\qquad
\cos(v,w)=0.8(0.28)+0.6(0.96)=0.8,
\]

\[
\cos(u,w)=u\cdot w=0.28<0.8.
\]

With an inclusive \(\geq0.8\) threshold, \(u\) is declared the same as (v), and (v) the same as (w), while (u) is not declared the same as (w).

- **Connected components:** the three nodes become one group by chaining through (v). That group asserts a transitive same-question relation contradicted by the direct (u,w) decision; a chain of locally similar texts can merge distinct questions.
- **Cliques:** the maximal positive-edge cliques are \({u,v\}\) and \({v,w\}\). They overlap, so they are not a partition. Forcing disjoint groups requires an arbitrary assignment or split and can make outcomes order-dependent.

For 5,000 questions, I would treat a high-precision similarity threshold as candidate-pair generation only. I would use a calibrated three-way pair decision (same / different / uncertain), verify representative pairs and all cluster conflicts, and use an explicit constrained clustering objective or human resolution for conflicts. I would not claim that connected components or overlapping cliques establish equivalence classes. A held-out set of independently labeled pairs and cluster-level audits would quantify false merges and splits.

## B.1 — Permutation-invariant encoders

The question in Figure T1.1, “How does X affect Y?”, and its role swap, “How does Y affect X?”, contain the same multiset of tokens. A permutation-invariant encoder has

\[
f(q)=g\!\left(\sum_i h(w_i)\right).
\]

A permutation leaves the sum unchanged, so both questions have exactly the same input to (g), and hence exactly the same vector. No downstream comparison of those vectors can separate this role swap.

The paper’s other near-miss types are constraint flip (“without labels” / “with labels”), goal swap (accuracy / speed), inquiry change (“how” / “why” or “can”), and direction flip (“improve” / “harm”). Each changes at least one token, so it changes the multiset and is **not forced** to be invisible by permutation invariance. But that does not guarantee separation: (h) may map the changed words to the same vector, the summed difference may cancel, or (g) may discard it. Thus role swap is necessarily invisible to this encoder form; the other four types are potentially visible, not guaranteed detectable.

## B.2 — Transformers, cross-encoders, and the trade-off

### (i) Mean-pooled transformer encoders

A transformer is not generally permutation-invariant: contextual token states depend on token positions and attention interactions, so swapping X and Y can change the hidden states and their mean. I would not expect reliable role-swap separation without targeted training and evaluation. The two questions share nearly all tokens, and mean pooling averages the role-bearing syntax and contextual differences with the shared topical content. Typical contrastive training pulls paraphrases, same-paper questions, or other positives together and pushes unrelated negatives apart; unless role swaps are explicitly supplied as hard negatives, that objective can reward topical similarity without teaching argument-role sensitivity. Some models may distinguish the swap, but the architecture alone does not ensure it.

### (ii) What a cross-encoder can represent

A cross-encoder jointly processes the pair. Cross-attention can compare “X” in one question with “X” in the other, bind each to its subject/object role, and model interactions among negation, direction, and outcome terms. It can therefore implement pair-specific evidence such as “the same entities occur, but their causal roles are reversed.” A cosine of two separately pooled vectors is a symmetric comparison of independent representations; it cannot condition the representation of one question on the particular tokens in the other at comparison time. A cross-encoder’s raw score may depend on input order, so an identity evaluation should test both orders and either report order effects or use a predeclared symmetrization.

### (iii) Topical retrieval versus near-miss sensitivity

Literature search wants same-topic questions and papers to remain close, including questions that differ in a consequential detail. Identity detection wants near-miss questions—often from the same topic and with almost the same words—to be separated. If training pulls all topical pairs together, it can also pull role-swapped or constraint-flipped questions together; if it pushes those near-misses apart, it may reduce retrieval of useful, closely related work. One cosine geometry and one operating point must negotiate these competing objectives; it cannot treat topical relatedness and identity as the same relation.

**Experiment and curve.** Create source-paper-grouped train, validation, and held-out test splits. For each anchor, collect (a) relevant prior papers / same-topic queries, (b) independently labeled paraphrases, and (c) same-topic near-misses for each of the five defined edit types. Match lengths where possible, keep all variants of a source question in one split, and hold out topics or edit templates to test transfer. Train the same encoder with a sweep of preregistered weights \(lambda\) on hard near-miss negatives versus topical retrieval positives. Select each identity threshold on validation only. For each \(lambda\), plot **x:** held-out literature-search Recall@10; **y:** held-out near-miss rejection rate at a fixed validation-set paraphrase-acceptance rate. Bootstrap source-question clusters for intervals. A Pareto curve with retrieval dropping as near-miss rejection rises would show the measured trade-off; no such curve or result is claimed here.

## C.1 — Length can masquerade as scope

Write an unnormalized embedding as \(z(q)=s(q)u(q)\), with \(\lVert u(q)\rVert=1\) and \(s(q)=\lVert z(q)\rVert\). The proposed constraint is \(s(q_b)<s(q_n)\) when \(q_b\) is broader than \(q_n\).

If every observed training pair happens to have (L(q_b)<L(q_n)), the model

\[
s(q)=L(q),\qquad u(q)=u_0\ \text{for any fixed unit vector }u_0,
\]

satisfies every strict training inequality. The scalar encodes token length only; the direction contains no question or scope information. More generally, if the only fact is the stated average ordering \(mathbb E[L(q_b)]<\mathbb E[L(q_n)]\), then \(s=L\) satisfies the **average** ordering, but that fact alone does not guarantee every pairwise inequality: individual pairs may reverse the length order. A claim that it satisfies every hard pairwise constraint therefore requires either pairwise length-order agreement in the observed set or a finite lookup/memorization mechanism for exceptions. Such memorization can fit a finite consistent training set without learning a scope rule and need not generalize.

**Control set.** Assemble independently verified, same-topic broad/narrow pairs with lengths counterbalanced: in one stratum broad is shorter; in the other broad is longer. Include meaning-preserving length variants so length can change without changing the scope label. Split by source paper and keep labels hidden from the encoder.

**Single statistic.** Let (C_{short}) and (C_{long}) be the proportions with (s(q_b)<s(q_n)) in the broad-shorter and broad-longer strata. Report the worst-stratum scope concordance

\[
T_{worst}=\min(C_{short},C_{long}).
\]

A pure (s=L) predictor can score 1 in the first stratum and 0 in the second, so (T_{worst}=0); a scope signal should order correctly in both. This directly tests whether the norm survives reversal of the length cue.

## C.2 — Box containment

For positive-volume boxes, the assignment defines

\[
P(B\subseteq A)=\frac{\\operatorname{Vol}(A\cap B)}{\\operatorname{Vol}(B)},\qquad
P(A\subseteq B)=\frac{\\operatorname{Vol}(A\cap B)}{\\operatorname{Vol}(A)}.
\]

### (i) Ratio identity

The assumed positive intersection volume makes the denominator ratio well-defined. Dividing the scores gives

\[
\frac{P(B\subseteq A)}{P(A\subseteq B)}
=\frac{\\operatorname{Vol}(A\cap B)/\\operatorname{Vol}(B)}
{\\operatorname{Vol}(A\cap B)/\\operatorname{Vol}(A)}
=\boxed{\frac{\\operatorname{Vol}(A)}{\\operatorname{Vol}(B)}}.
\]

### (ii) Direction and magnitude

The direction of the relative containment scores is decided solely by the volume ratio: if \(\operatorname{Vol}(A)>\\operatorname{Vol}(B)\), then (P(B\subseteq A)>P(A\subseteq B)), so the larger box is the proposed broader container. The magnitude (P(B\subseteq A)) additionally gives the fraction of B’s volume overlapping A (equivalently, under uniform volume measure, the fraction of B covered by A). A volume ordering alone does not guarantee geometric containment.

### (iii) Large volume ratio, low overlap

In \(mathbb R^2\), choose

\[
A=[0,10]\times[0,10],\qquad
B=[9.95,10.95]\times[0,1].
\]

Then \(\operatorname{Vol}(A)=100\), \(\operatorname{Vol}(B)=1\), and \(\operatorname{Vol}(A)\ge10\\operatorname{Vol}(B)\). Their intersection is \([9.95,10]\times[0,1]\), with positive area (0.05). Thus

\[
P(B\subseteq A)=\frac{0.05}{1}=\boxed{0.05\le0.1}.
\]

A is much larger, but only a thin sliver of B lies inside it. The containment direction favors A as broader, while the low score warns that A does not actually cover most of B.

## C.3 — A product geometry for topic, identity, and scope

Use a shared product representation

\[
\Phi(q)=\big(u_T(q),u_I(q),r(q)\big)
\in \mathbb S^{d_T-1}\times\mathbb S^{d_I-1}\times\mathbb R_{>0}.
\]

- (u_T(q)) is a unit topic direction, trained to preserve broad topical relatedness. Its cosine answers **“Are these questions in the same research area?”** It should remain high for same-topic near-misses.
- (u_I(q)) is a separate unit question-identity direction, trained on verified identity pairs and hard negatives for role, constraint, goal, inquiry, and direction changes. Its angular distance answers **“Do these ask the same question?”**
- (r(q)>0) is a scope radius, with smaller radius meaning broader scope (as in Fig. T1.2). Compare it only after the topic component establishes that the questions concern the same area. If \(u_T(q_1)\) and \(u_T(q_2)\) are sufficiently close and \(log r(q_1)<\log r(q_2)\) by a margin, the geometry predicts (q_1) is broader; scope-equivalent paraphrases should have similar radii.

For an operational identity decision, require topic compatibility, identity-angle similarity above a validation-locked threshold, and scope-radius difference within a locked tolerance. Topic similarity alone does not imply identity; radius ordering alone does not compare unrelated topics. The component losses and thresholds must be trained/selected without held-out labels, and all three outputs come from this single shared product representation.

**Falsifiable prediction and test.** On held-out, length-matched same-topic pairs with independent labels, the identity component will reject consequential near-misses at a higher rate than a single topic cosine while retaining paraphrase acceptance; the topic component will still rank same-topic near-misses above unrelated-topic controls. Test on source-paper-disjoint years or topics, compare with a single-cosine baseline at the same validation-locked paraphrase acceptance, and use source-question-clustered paired intervals. Failure to improve held-out near-miss rejection without losing topical retrieval would falsify the proposed factorization’s practical advantage. No result is asserted.
