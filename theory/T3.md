## T3.A — Length Bias

Let (L_1) and (L_2) denote the lengths of two proposals, and let (\varepsilon_1) and (\varepsilon_2) be independent continuous noise terms. The scorer assigns each proposal

[
S_i=L_i+\varepsilon_i.
]

Because experts prefer the longer proposal in 65% of pairwise battles, a scorer that primarily follows length will agree with the experts in approximately 65% of battles when the noise is sufficiently small. Thus, agreement above 50% does not demonstrate that the scorer understands research quality or novelty; it may simply reproduce the length preference.

Remedy 1 — Length-matched comparisons: Compare proposals of approximately equal length. This removes the direct advantage of length, although it may reduce the sample size and exclude naturally different-length proposals.

Remedy 2 — Length-only baseline: Evaluate a model using only proposal length and compare its accuracy with the proposed scorer. If their performance is similar, there is little evidence that the proposed scorer adds useful information beyond length. However, this comparison does not rule out other confounding factors.
## T3.B — Systems versus Ideas

The scorer outputs the system indicator (1 for system A and 0 for system B) plus independent noise uniformly distributed between -0.4 and 0.4.

**Cross-system battles:** The difference between the system indicators is 1, while the maximum possible difference in noise is 0.8. Therefore, the scorer always ranks the system A idea higher. Experts prefer A in 80% of these battles, so agreement is 80%.

**Within-system battles:** Both ideas have the same system indicator, which cancels during comparison. Since the noise terms are independent and identically distributed, either idea is equally likely to receive the higher score. Agreement is therefore 50%.

**Overall agreement:**

\[
A_{\text{overall}}=(0.60\times0.80)+(0.40\times0.50)=0.68
\]

Overall agreement is **68%**.

**System-demeaned accuracy:** This measures agreement after removing the system-level advantage. It is equivalent to within-system accuracy here:

\[
A_{\text{demeaned}}=50\%.
\]

The within-system result answers whether the scorer can select the better idea from comparable systems. At 50%, it demonstrates no ability beyond system identity.

## T3.C — Re-asking

### (i) Share of realised questions that were already asked

Given:

- P(already asked) = 0.30
- P(realised later | already asked) = 0.10
- P(realised later | not already asked) = 0.004

Using Bayes' theorem:

\[
P(A\mid R)=\frac{P(R\mid A)P(A)}
{P(R\mid A)P(A)+P(R\mid \neg A)P(\neg A)}
\]

\[
=\frac{0.10\times0.30}
{(0.10\times0.30)+(0.004\times0.70)}
\]

\[
=\frac{0.030}{0.0328}\approx0.9146
\]

Therefore, approximately **91.46% of realised questions had already been asked before the cutoff**.

### (ii) A misleading leaderboard

Consider a generator that produces 100 questions, all of which were already asked before the cutoff. If 10 of these questions are realised again later, its later-realisation rate is:

\[
\frac{10}{100}\times100=10\%.
\]

It achieves a 10% leaderboard rate while producing **zero net-new questions**, because every generated question was already present before the cutoff.

### (iii) Corrected metrics

A corrected metric is the **net-new realisation rate**:

\[
P(\text{realised later}\mid\text{not already asked}).
\]

For the given data, this is:

\[
\boxed{0.004=0.4\%}
\]

This excludes questions that were already asked before the cutoff and measures the later realisation of genuinely new questions.

The before/after correction compares matches found after the cutoff with matches found before it. It controls for the background tendency of a question to match existing or future literature, including recurring research interests. In contrast, the net-new realisation rate directly excludes previously asked questions. The two corrections address different biases and should be reported together.

## T3.D — Who Wrote It?

The scorer prefers human-written research questions over language-model-written questions in 85% of pairs. This preference may reflect authorship rather than actual research quality. I would conduct the following experiments:

**1. Same-model rewriting experiment:** Rewrite both the human-written and AI-written questions using the same language model and prompt. If the scorer's preference for human-written questions disappears, its original preference may have been driven by writing style or authorship cues rather than quality. If it persists, there may be meaningful differences in the underlying ideas.

**2. Model-family swap experiment:** Generate questions from the same inputs using different language-model families, then compare them with human-written questions. If the scorer consistently prefers human questions regardless of the generating model, it may be detecting authorship-related features. If its preferences vary substantially across model families, it may be sensitive to model-specific writing styles.

**3. Same-authorship pair experiment:** Compare pairs in which both questions are human-written or both are AI-generated. Obtain independent human quality judgments without revealing authorship. If the scorer agrees with these quality judgments within both groups, this supports its ability to measure quality. If it mainly prefers one question based on its author type, its score is likely affected by authorship bias.

**4. Blinded human evaluation:** Ask independent reviewers to compare the quality of the two questions without revealing which was written by a human or a model. If the scorer agrees with blinded human judgments, that supports a quality interpretation. If it disagrees while continuing to prefer human-written questions, authorship bias is a plausible explanation.

These experiments separate genuine differences in research-question quality from stylistic and authorship signals.

## T3.E — Contamination and Power

### (i) Detecting memorisation versus genuine judgement

A language-model scorer trained on data up to date T may predict conference acceptance by remembering papers, titles, abstracts, or acceptance outcomes rather than evaluating research quality. I would use two tests:

**1. Temporal holdout test:** Evaluate the scorer separately on papers whose acceptance decisions occurred before and after the model's training cutoff. Compare its performance across the two groups. If performance is substantially higher on pre-cutoff papers, this suggests that the scorer may be benefiting from memorised information. Strong performance on genuinely post-cutoff papers provides better evidence of generalisable judgement.

**2. Counterfactual and metadata intervention test:** Keep a paper's research content unchanged while removing or replacing identifying information, such as its title, authors, institution, venue, and publication year. Also test paraphrased versions of its abstract. If the acceptance prediction changes substantially when identifying details are altered, the scorer may rely on memorised metadata or recognition cues. If predictions remain relatively stable, this supports the interpretation that it evaluates the research content.

Together, these tests help distinguish recall of known acceptance outcomes from generalisable assessment of research quality.

### (ii) Statistical power and confidence interval

The scorer achieves 54% accuracy on independent pairs, compared with the 50% chance-level null hypothesis. The effect to detect is therefore:

\[
p_1-p_0=0.54-0.50=0.04
\]

**Required sample size for 80% power**

For a two-sided test with significance level \(\alpha=0.05\), power \(=0.80\), and null accuracy \(p_0=0.50\), use the normal approximation:

\[
n \approx \frac{(z_{0.975}\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)}
+z_{0.80}\sqrt{p_1(1-p_1)})^2}
{(p_1-p_0)^2}
\]

Using \(z_{0.975}=1.96\), \(z_{0.80}=0.84\), \(p_0=0.50\), and \(p_1=0.54\):

\[
n \approx \frac{(1.96\sqrt{0.25}+0.84\sqrt{0.2484})^2}{0.04^2}
\]

\[
n \approx 1,225
\]

Therefore, approximately **1,225 independent pairs** are required to achieve 80% power.

**95% confidence interval half-width with 400 pairs**

Using the observed accuracy of 54%:

\[
SE=\sqrt{\frac{0.54(1-0.54)}{400}}
\]

\[
SE\approx 0.0249
\]

The 95% confidence interval half-width is:

\[
1.96\times SE \approx 0.0488
\]

Thus, the half-width is approximately **4.9 percentage points**. The approximate confidence interval is:

\[
54\%\pm 4.9\%=[49.1\%,58.9\%]
\]

This interval includes 50%, so 400 pairs would not provide strong evidence that the scorer performs above chance.

**Two ways to improve power without collecting new labels**

1. **Use paired or blocked comparisons:** Match comparisons by topic, research area, or other relevant characteristics, then analyse within-block differences. This can reduce irrelevant variation and improve statistical power. However, poor matching or treating correlated observations as independent can produce misleadingly narrow confidence intervals.

2. **Use a pre-registered one-sided test when scientifically justified:** If the only relevant hypothesis is that accuracy exceeds 50%, a one-sided test can provide greater power than a two-sided test at the same significance level. However, choosing a one-sided test after observing the results inflates the false-positive risk, and it cannot detect performance significantly below chance.

These methods can improve the efficiency of the analysis, but they do not replace independent evaluation or justify overstating a small effect.
