## T2.A.1 — Grouping the Ten Novelty Measures

The ten measures capture different aspects of novelty. They can be grouped according to the question about the world that each measure attempts to answer.

Group 1: How different is the idea from previous research?

Measures 1, 5 and 6

* Measure 1 — Maximum cosine distance: It measures how dissimilar an idea is from its closest earlier paper. It may wrongly reward an idea that is expressed differently or belongs to an unrelated topic, even if its contribution is not genuinely new.
* Measure 5 — Core-facet matching: It considers an idea non-novel only when an earlier paper matches every core facet. It may wrongly reward a minor change to one facet even when most of the contribution has already been established.
* Measure 6 — Per-feature dissimilarity: It measures differences from a selected population of documents across individual features. It may wrongly reward differences in irrelevant features or depend heavily on the choice of comparison population.

These measures focus on how an idea differs from existing work, either through overall similarity, matching of important facets or feature-level differences.

Group 2: How statistically unusual is the idea?

Measures 2 and 9

* Measure 2 — Neighbourhood-density percentile: It measures how unusual the local embedding density of an idea is compared with its neighbours. It may wrongly reward an isolated or poorly represented idea caused by embedding errors rather than genuine novelty.
* Measure 9 — Unseen bigram share: It measures the proportion of word pairs that have not appeared in earlier literature. It may wrongly reward newly coined terminology or unusual wording without any new research contribution.

These measures focus on statistical or linguistic rarity rather than directly evaluating the importance of an idea.

Group 3: How conceptually new or differently connected is the idea?

Measures 3 and 4

* Measure 3 — Share outside the topic’s linear span: It measures how much of an idea’s embedding lies outside the span of its topic’s literature. It may wrongly reward noise or an embedding direction unrelated to a meaningful research contribution.
* Measure 4 — Historical co-occurrence tour length: It measures the length of the shortest tour through an idea’s concepts in a historical co-occurrence network. It may wrongly reward disconnected or obscure combinations of concepts that do not form a meaningful contribution.

These measures focus on whether an idea introduces concepts or combinations that are not well represented by existing relationships in the literature.

Group 4: Does the idea receive independent or future validation?

Measures 7 and 10

* Measure 7 — Future similarity: It checks whether papers published after the idea do something similar. It may wrongly reward ideas that later become popular or independently rediscovered, while undervaluing useful ideas that are never adopted.
* Measure 10 — Uptake: It measures the subsequent attention or influence received by an idea. It may wrongly reward popularity caused by author reputation, institutional resources, publication venue or research trends rather than novelty itself.

These measures use later research behaviour and attention as evidence, rather than measuring novelty solely from the idea and its prior literature.

Group 5: Can a language model judge the idea as novel?

Measure 8

* Measure 8 — Language-model novelty judgment: It uses a language model to assess the novelty of an idea. It may wrongly reward persuasive writing, confident claims or prompt-specific patterns instead of genuine originality.

This measure relies on a model’s interpretation of novelty and may inherit biases from its training data and evaluation instructions.

Overall observation: These measures do not all measure the same property. Some measure distance from prior work, others measure rarity or conceptual combinations, while others use future uptake or model judgments. Their scores should therefore be interpreted according to the particular question each measure answers.

## T2.A.2 — Why Two Published Claims Can Both Be True

The claims “distance from prior work measures novelty” and “distance from prior work does not predict reviewers’ novelty scores” can both be true because they refer to different aspects of novelty.

Distance from prior work measures how different an idea is from existing research. It can therefore capture one dimension of novelty, namely the degree of difference from previously published work.

However, reviewers may consider more than distance when judging novelty. They may also consider the significance of the contribution, the quality of the evidence, the clarity of the idea and whether the contribution is meaningful. An idea can be very different from previous work but still be weak or unimportant. Similarly, an idea can build closely on earlier research while making a valuable contribution.

In addition, embedding-based distance measures may not capture semantic overlap accurately, and reviewer judgments can contain noise and differences in interpretation.

Therefore, distance from prior work can be a meaningful measure of one aspect of novelty without necessarily predicting reviewers’ overall novelty scores. Both published claims can be valid because they evaluate different things.
## T2.B.1 — Distributed Coverage

The claim embeddings (c_1,c_2,\ldots,c_m) are orthonormal unit vectors. Therefore, the cosine similarity between a claim and itself is 1, while the similarity between two different claims is 0.

In the distributed coverage setting, the paper pool is (P={c_1,c_2,\ldots,c_j}). The whole idea is represented by

\[
x=\frac{1}{\sqrt m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_i.
\]

For every paper embedding (c_i) in the pool, its cosine similarity with the whole idea is (1/\sqrt m). Hence,

\[
Y_D=1-\max_{p\in P}\cos(x,p)
=1-\frac{1}{\sqrt m}.
\]

For the claim-level measure (N), the first (j) claims have maximum similarity 1 because they are present in the pool. The remaining (m-j) claims have maximum similarity 0 because they are orthogonal to all papers in the pool. Thus,

\[
N_D=1-\frac{1}{m}\left(j\cdot1+(m-j)\cdot0\right)
=1-\frac jm.
\]

As (j) increases, (N_D) strictly decreases, whereas (Y_D) remains constant. Therefore, (N) satisfies the gradient axiom under distributed coverage, but (Y) does not.

## T2.B.2 — Concentrated Coverage

In concentrated coverage, a single paper represents the first (j) claims. Its embedding is

\[
p=\frac{1}{\sqrt j}\sum_{i=1}^{j}c_i.
\]

To calculate whole-idea novelty, we first find the cosine similarity between the whole idea (x) and the paper (p). Since both are unit vectors,

\[
\cos(x,p)=x^\top p
=\frac{j}{\sqrt m\sqrt j}
=\sqrt{\frac jm}.
\]

Therefore,

\[
Y_C=1-\sqrt{\frac jm}.
\]

For claim-level novelty, each of the first (j) claims has cosine similarity (1/\sqrt j) with the paper. The remaining (m-j) claims are orthogonal to the paper and have similarity zero.

Thus,

\[
N_C=1-\frac1m\left(j\cdot\frac1{\sqrt j}+(m-j)\cdot0\right).
\]

Simplifying,

\[
N_C=1-\frac{\sqrt j}{m}.
\]

Hence, the concentrated coverage measures are:

\[
\boxed{Y_C=1-\sqrt{\frac jm}}
\]

\[
\boxed{N_C=1-\frac{\sqrt j}{m}}
\]

## T2.B.3 — Testing the Concentration Axiom

The concentration axiom requires the novelty score under concentrated coverage to be strictly lower than the score under distributed coverage for every (1<j\leq m).

For the whole-idea measure (Y), we compare:

\[
Y_C-Y_D=\left(1-\sqrt{\frac jm}\right)-\left(1-\frac1{\sqrt m}\right).
\]

Simplifying,

\[
Y_C-Y_D=-\frac{\sqrt j-1}{\sqrt m}.
\]

Since (j>1), we have (\sqrt j-1>0). Therefore,

\[
Y_C-Y_D<0.
\]

Hence, (Y) satisfies the concentration axiom.

For the claim-level measure (N), we compare:

\[
N_C-N_D=\left(1-\frac{\sqrt j}{m}\right)-\left(1-\frac jm\right).
\]

Simplifying,

\[
N_C-N_D=\frac{j-\sqrt j}{m}.
\]

Since (j>1), we have (j>\sqrt j). Therefore,

\[
N_C-N_D>0.
\]

Hence, (N) does not satisfy the concentration axiom.

Conclusion: The whole-idea measure (Y) satisfies the concentration axiom, whereas the claim-level measure (N) violates it.
## T2.B.4 — Constructing a Combined Novelty Measure

The previous results show that (Y) satisfies the concentration axiom but fails the gradient axiom. In contrast, (N) satisfies the gradient axiom but fails the concentration axiom.

To satisfy both axioms, we define a weighted combination:

\[
M_\alpha=\alpha Y+(1-\alpha)N,
\]

where (0\leq\alpha\leq1).

Gradient axiom

Under distributed coverage,

\[
M_D=
\alpha\left(1-\frac1{\sqrt m}\right)
+(1-\alpha)\left(1-\frac jm\right).
\]

For fixed (m), increasing (j) strictly decreases this expression whenever (\alpha<1). Therefore, the gradient axiom requires:

\[
\alpha<1.
\]

Concentration axiom

The difference between concentrated and distributed scores is:

\[
M_C-M_D=\alpha(Y_C-Y_D)+(1-\alpha)(N_C-N_D).
\]

Substituting the results from B.3 gives:

\[
M_C-M_D=
(\sqrt j-1)
\left[
-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt m}
+\frac{(1-\alpha)\sqrt j}{m}
\right].
\]

For this difference to be strictly negative for every (1<j\leq m), we require:

\[
\alpha>
\frac{\sqrt{j/m}}{1+\sqrt{j/m}}.
\]

The right-hand side is at most (1/2), because (j\leq m). Therefore, the condition for all permitted (j) is:

\[
\alpha>\frac12.
\]

Combining both axioms, we obtain:

\[
\boxed{\frac12<\alpha<1}.
\]

We can choose (\alpha=0.75), giving:

\[
\boxed{M=0.75Y+0.25N}.
\]

This combined measure satisfies both the gradient and concentration axioms in the given orthonormal model.

Adding papers

When the paper pool grows, the maximum similarity for each measure cannot decrease, because the enlarged pool contains all previously available papers. Therefore, both (Y) and (N) cannot increase. Since (M) is a weighted sum with non-negative weights, adding papers cannot increase (M).

Paraphrase invariance

If paraphrasing preserves the embedding of every claim, then the claim embeddings and the whole-idea embedding remain unchanged. Consequently, (Y), (N), and their combined measure (M) also remain unchanged.

Limitations in real research corpora

The mathematical model assumes orthogonal claims and ideal embeddings. In real research corpora, claims may overlap semantically, claim extraction may be inconsistent, and cosine similarity may capture topical similarity rather than genuine research coverage. These factors can affect whether the two axioms hold in practice.
## T2.C.1 — Split-Half Reliability

Let (\sigma_T^2) represent the true-score variance and (\sigma_E^2) represent the error variance in each half-panel. Under the classical parallel-measurement model, the two half-panel means have independent errors of equal variance.

The correlation between the two half-panel means is:

\[
r=\frac{\sigma_T^2}{\sigma_T^2+\sigma_E^2}=0.36.
\]

The full-panel mean averages the two halves. Since their errors are independent, averaging reduces the error variance to (\sigma_E^2/2).

Therefore, the reliability of the full-panel mean is:

\[
R_{\text{full}}=
\frac{\sigma_T^2}{\sigma_T^2+\sigma_E^2/2}.
\]

Using (r=\sigma_T^2/(\sigma_T^2+\sigma_E^2)), this can be rewritten as:

\[
R_{\text{full}}=\frac{2r}{1+r}
=\frac{2(0.36)}{1+0.36}
=\boxed{0.5294}.
\]

The largest correlation any predictor can achieve with the full-panel mean is the square root of its reliability, assuming the predictor perfectly captures the true score:

\[
r_{\max}=\sqrt{R_{\text{full}}}=\sqrt{0.5294118}=\boxed{0.7276\text{ (approximately)}}.
\]

Thus, even a perfect predictor of true novelty cannot correlate with the noisy full-panel mean above approximately 0.728 under these assumptions.

To measure the association between the predictor and novelty after controlling for the overall recommendation, we use the partial correlation formula:

r_{PN\cdot Y}=

Substituting the given values:

## T2.C.3 — Separating Novelty from Quality

A benchmark that compares papers rated as highly novel by every reviewer with papers rated as not novel by every reviewer cannot distinguish novelty from quality. This is because the two groups may also differ substantially in overall paper quality. Papers judged as highly novel may receive stronger overall recommendations, while papers judged as not novel may receive weaker recommendations. Therefore, a score that separates these groups could simply be measuring quality rather than novelty.

To separate novelty from quality, I would design a matched comparison between papers with similar overall recommendation or quality ratings but different reviewer novelty ratings.

For example, papers could be grouped into pairs within the same research area and with similar overall recommendation scores. Within each pair, one paper would have a higher novelty rating than the other. The benchmark would then test whether the novelty measure correctly ranks the more novel paper higher.

This comparison controls for broad differences in quality and tests whether the score captures novelty beyond overall reviewer evaluation. The matching procedure and the acceptable difference in recommendation scores should be specified before evaluating the novelty measure.

## T2 — Final Answer Sheet

- **A.1 groups:** (1, 5, 6) distance from prior work; (2, 9) statistical/linguistic rarity; (3, 4) conceptual span or combinations; (7, 10) future validation/uptake; (8) language-model judgment. Each grouping and one failure mode per measure are discussed above.
- **A.2:** Distance can measure one dimension of novelty while failing to predict reviewers’ overall novelty judgments, which also reflect significance, evidence, clarity and noisy reviewer criteria.
- **B.1 distributed:** \(Y_D=1-1/\sqrt m\), \(N_D=1-j/m\); only \(N_D\) strictly decreases with \(j\).
- **B.2 concentrated:** \(Y_C=1-\sqrt{j/m}\), \(N_C=1-\sqrt j/m\).
- **B.3 concentration axiom:** \(Y_C-Y_D=-(\sqrt j-1)/\sqrt m<0\), so \(Y\) satisfies it. \(N_C-N_D=(j-\sqrt j)/m>0\), so \(N\) violates it.
- **B.4 combined measure:** \(M_\alpha=\alpha Y+(1-\alpha)N\), with \(1/2<\alpha<1\); e.g. \(\alpha=0.75\). It is paraphrase-invariant when embeddings are unchanged and nonincreasing as papers are added. These are model properties, not empirical validation.
- **C.1:** Full-panel reliability \(2r/(1+r)=0.5294\); maximum predictor correlation \(\sqrt{0.5294}=0.7276\), under the parallel-half, independent equal-error assumptions.
- **C.2:** Partial correlation \(r_{PN\cdot Y}=0.181\) (approximately), a conditional association, not a causal estimate.
- **C.3:** Match or stratify papers by quality/recommendation and topic while comparing novelty ratings; use discordant novelty cases within quality strata. Perfectly aligned novelty and quality labels do not identify separate effects.
