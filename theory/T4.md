T4 — The Geometry of Grounding

T4.A — Inside or Outside?

Task A.1

The affine span of the (k) affinely independent input vectors has dimension (k-1). Let (P) be the orthogonal projection onto its direction space.

Since (\delta=q-c) is an isotropic Gaussian vector, its components have equal expected squared magnitude in every orthogonal direction. Therefore, the expected fraction of its squared norm lying in the ((k-1))-dimensional direction space is

[
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|P\delta|^2}{|\delta|^2}\right]
=\frac{k-1}{d}.
]

For (k=30) and (d=768),

[
\frac{30-1}{768}=\frac{29}{768}\approx 0.03776.
]

Thus, the expected in-plane share is approximately 3.78%.

This shows that, even under completely isotropic noise, only a small fraction of the question vector’s squared displacement is expected to lie in the affine span of its inputs. Consequently, in-plane measures such as depth, barycentric coordinates and convex-hull membership capture only a small component of the geometry and may be unstable or uninformative in high dimensions.

A real question vector will essentially never lie inside the convex hull of its inputs. The convex hull is contained in a ((k-1))-dimensional affine subspace, while the embedding space has dimension (768). When (k-1<768), this subspace has zero volume in the full embedding space. A continuously distributed vector therefore has probability zero of lying exactly inside it.

Task A.2

If real papers have an in-plane share close to the isotropic prediction of 3.78%, the in-plane share alone does not provide evidence of meaningful grounding.

Instead, I would measure the off-plane residual structure, including:

1. The magnitude and direction of the off-plane component of the question vector.
2. Its similarity to the individual input vectors and to their centroid.
3. Whether the residual contains information about the research question beyond what is explained by the input set.

I would compare these measurements against an isotropic Gaussian null baseline matched to the observed embedding dimension, input count and vector norms. I would also use shuffled-input controls, pairing each question with another paper’s inputs, and random same-topic questions as additional baselines.

A grounding measure would be informative only if real question–input relationships show a reproducible difference from these controls. This avoids treating a geometric property expected by chance as evidence of genuine grounding.

T4.B — Noise

Task B

The observed angle between the early and final questions is

[
\theta_{\text{obs}}=50^\circ.
]

The self-agreement angles are (28^\circ) and (35^\circ). Using the attenuation correction,

[
\cos\theta_{\text{true}}
\approx
\frac{\cos\theta_{\text{obs}}}
{\sqrt{r_{\text{early}}r_{\text{final}}}},
]

where

[
r_{\text{early}}=\cos 28^\circ,
\qquad
r_{\text{final}}=\cos 35^\circ.
]

Substituting,

[
\cos\theta_{\text{true}}
\approx
\frac{\cos 50^\circ}
{\sqrt{\cos 28^\circ\cos 35^\circ}}
]

[
\approx \frac{0.6428}{\sqrt{0.8829\times0.8192}}
\approx 0.755.
]

Therefore,

[
\theta_{\text{true}}
\approx \cos^{-1}(0.755)
\approx 40.9^\circ.
]

Estimated true angle: approximately (40.9^\circ).

Assumption: The extraction noise in the early and final question representations is independent, approximately isotropic, and does not systematically favour any direction. The self-agreement values must reliably estimate the corresponding noise attenuation.

Possible failure: If extraction noise is correlated across prompts or systematically changes particular semantic features, self-agreement will not correctly estimate the attenuation. The correction may then overestimate or underestimate the true angle.

T4.C — Confounds and Incentives

Task C — Confounds

A grounding score may distinguish strong-venue papers from workshop papers because of factors unrelated to how their research questions are constructed.

Confound 1: Differences in research fields and topics

Strong venues and workshops may publish papers from different research areas. Some fields naturally reuse established terminology and have denser semantic connections between papers and their inputs. This can increase proximity scores without indicating better grounding.

Control: Compare papers within the same research field and topic. Use matched samples with similar input sets or topic-level fixed effects to determine whether the venue difference remains after controlling for topic.

Confound 2: Differences in writing quality and paper length

Strong-venue papers may have more polished abstracts, clearer explanations and more detailed descriptions of prior work. Their wording may therefore overlap more closely with the input papers, artificially increasing semantic proximity.

Control: Match papers by abstract length and control for writing-related features. Additionally, evaluate standardized question representations extracted using the same procedure, rather than comparing entire abstracts.

A venue-related difference should be considered evidence of grounding quality only if it remains after these controls.

Task D — Proximity, Novelty and Impact

(i) How a generator could maximise proximity without producing novelty

A generator could maximise a proximity-based score by copying or closely paraphrasing the research questions, titles or abstracts of the input papers. It could also combine existing statements with minor wording changes and present them as a new research question.

Such a question would remain semantically close to the inputs and achieve a high proximity score, even though it introduces no genuinely new research direction.

(ii) A guard against this strategy and its cost

I would introduce a novelty guard that compares the generated question against both the input papers and a broader corpus of existing research questions. The guard would identify near-duplicates, copied claims and questions that differ only through superficial paraphrasing.

The generator would need to demonstrate a meaningful research gap, a new relationship between existing ideas or a testable distinction that is not already addressed by the inputs.

Cost: This guard may penalize legitimate incremental research, useful terminology reuse and interdisciplinary combinations. It can also produce false positives by treating genuinely new questions as too similar to existing work. Additional corpus searches and human review would increase computational and evaluation costs.

(iii) Why impact and novelty should be reported separately

Impact and novelty measure different properties of research.

* Impact measures the relevance, usefulness or influence of a research question.
* Novelty measures how substantially the question differs from existing knowledge and prior research.

A question can be highly relevant and influential while contributing little that is new. Conversely, a highly novel question may initially have limited impact or attract few citations.

Combining both properties into one score can hide these differences and allow high impact to compensate for low novelty, or vice versa.

Therefore, an evaluator should report impact and novelty on separate axes. This provides a clearer assessment of research quality, exposes trade-offs and prevents a generator from appearing successful merely by optimising one property.
