General instructions
  This competition asks you to build a space of research questions, in
  which one can read off whether a question is new, whether it is well
  grounded in what it builds on, whether it is likely to be good, how varied
  a set of questions is and how all of this changes as science moves on.
  Theory paper hands you the tools and shows you the traps. Experiment
  paper makes you measure. Main Problem asks you to put it together.


How you are assessed

  • Admission is decided by the Main Problem. Theory and Experiment papers
    lead you to it step by step: each problem builds a tool, a dataset or an insight that
    the Main Problem needs.
  • Full marks in Theory and Experiment do not guarantee admission; several tasks are
    easy on purpose. A strong Main Problem with a patchy Theory and Experiment
    record can be admitted.
  • Theory and Experiment marks break ties and help match you to a project.
  • Required work: all of T1–T5, any three of E1–E5, E6 and the Main Problem.
    (Starting the Main Problem early is encouraged.)


Rules

R1. AI assistants are allowed for code, reading and brainstorming. Log every
    substantive use in AI_LOG.md: the tool, the task and what you checked. Include at
    least one case where the assistant was wrong and how you caught it. Undisclosed
    use disqualifies the submission while honest reporting would be rewarded points.
R2. Pre-register before you test. Before evaluating anything on a test set, commit
    PREREG.md with your hypotheses, metrics, test sets and pass criteria. The git history
    is the timestamp. Results that contradict your pre-registration are welcome; silent
    edits to it are not.
R3. Report failures. A well-measured negative result scores as well as a positive
    one. Every abandoned idea gets one dated line in NOTEBOOK.md saying why it was
    dropped.
R4. Respect time. “Novelty at time t” may use only literature dated strictly before t.
    Any claim that rests on a language model reading papers must be confirmed on
    papers posted after that model’s training cutoff. State the cutoff you assume and
    why.
R5. Compute. Everything is designed for one GPU with 24 GB of memory. A small
    tier (embedding models of about 0.6B parameters, language models up to about
    2B, free notebook GPUs) is acceptable everywhere; say which tier you used. Paid

Notation and definitions

   Definition (Research question, inputs, time)
   A research question q is the one-sentence question that a paper or an AI system,
   sets out to answer. Its inputs R = {r1 , . . . , rk } are the papers it is built from: a
   paper’s references or the papers given to a generator. The time t is when q is
   posed; “the literature at t” means the papers dated strictly before t.


   Definition (Relations between questions)

      • Identity q1 ≡ q2 : a complete answer to either question is a complete answer
        to the other.
      • Subsumption q1 ⪰ q2 (“q1 is broader”): every complete answer to q1 yields
        a complete answer to q2 .
      • Paraphrase: an identical question in other words. A minimal-edit paraphrase
        keeps at least 75 % of the words.
      • Near-miss: a small edit that changes what is asked. Types: role swap (“how

          → “with labels”), goal swap (accuracy → speed), inquiry change (“how does”
          → “why does” or “can we”), direction flip (“improve” → “harm”).
      • Facets: purpose (what problem), mechanism (how), evaluation (how success
        is judged), domain (where).

   Definition (Time and outcomes)

      • Already asked at t: some paper dated before t asks a question identical to
        q.
      • Addressed by the inputs: the evidence contained in R already answers q.
      • Realised later: a paper dated after t asks a question identical to q. Net-
        new realisation additionally requires that q was not already asked at t and
        that the later paper neither cites nor shares an author with the source of q.
      • Uptake: a claim that is new at t and is later taken up by author-disjoint
        work.
      • Provenance: who wrote q, a person or a language model.
      • Lockbox: a test set that is evaluated exactly once, after every choice has
        been frozen.

Object of this competition

Throughout the paper you work towards a representation

                                            Φ(q, R, t)

of a research question q, the inputs R it is built from and the time t at which it is posed.
From Φ one should be able to read: whether two questions are the same and which one
is broader; how novel q is at time t, in every sense of “novel” that matters; whether q is
grounded in R; how good q is likely to be judged; how diverse a set of questions is; and
how all of these readings change as later literature arrives. A good Φ is

   • invariant to what should not matter: paraphrase, writing style, who or what wrote
     the question, its length, the order of R;
   • sensitive to what should: near-miss edits, edits of a single facet, a change of scope,
     a change of R and the passage of time;
   • stable where science is stable: a model of how good questions are formed, learned
     on one period or field, should transfer to another.

T1 Identity, scope and limits of 1 vector
10 points                                                                 Difficulty: ⋆⋆

Recommended Read first: [R5], [R16].

A sentence embedding f maps a text to a unit vector in Rd and two texts are compared
by the cosine of their vectors. Research questions need more than “similar or not”. We
must decide whether two questions are the same, whether one is broader than the other
and whether a small edit has changed what is asked. This problem asks what a single
vector can and cannot do.


§T1.A Relations between questions                                              (2.0 points)


   Definition (Answer sets)
  Model a question q by the set A(q) of its complete answers and assume this set is
  closed upwards under entailment: any statement that entails a complete answer to
  q is itself a complete answer to q.


  Task A.1                                                                      1.0 pt

     (i) Show that q1 ⪰ q2 holds if and only if A(q1 ) ⊆ A(q2 ).
    (ii) Deduce that ≡ is an equivalence relation, that ⪰ is a preorder and that the
         symmetric part of ⪰ is ≡.
   (iii) Give two realistic machine-learning research questions with q1 ⪰ q2 but not
         q2 ⪰ q1 .
   (iv) Questions of the form “Can X improve Y ?” are completely answered by
        a single positive example. Using q1 = “Can data augmentation improve
        contrastive pre-training?” and q2 = “Can colour jitter improve contrastive
        pre-training?”, explain what goes wrong with the intuition “the broader
        question subsumes the narrower one”.


  Task A.2                                                                      1.0 pt
  Fix τ = 0.8. Construct unit vectors u, v, w ∈ R2 with cos(u, v) ≥ τ and cos(v, w) ≥ τ
  but cos(u, w) < τ . A pool of 5 000 generated questions is to be deduplicated by
  declaring two questions “the same” whenever their cosine is at least τ . Compare
  two ways of forming groups from these pairwise decisions: connected components of
  the “same” graph and cliques of it. What goes wrong in each case and what would
  you do instead?

T2 What does “novel” mean?
10 points                                                                     Difficulty: ⋆⋆

Recommended Read first: [R1], [R5], [R4], [R8], [R2].

“Novelty” is measured in dozens of ways and published claims about it often seem to
contradict one another. You will first sort the measures, then study one of them exactly
in a toy model and finally ask how well any measure could agree with human reviewers.


§T2.A A zoo of measures                                                           (2.0 points)

Consider these ten measures of the novelty of an idea x at time t:

   1. One minus the largest cosine between x and any earlier paper;
   2. Percentile of x’s neighbourhood density among the densities of its neighbours [R7];
   3. Share of x’s embedding that lies outside the linear span of its topic’s literature [R4];
   4. Length of the shortest tour through x’s concepts in a historical co-occurrence
      network [R10];
   5. Rule “x is not novel if and only if one earlier paper matches it on every core facet”
      [R5];
   6. Per-feature dissimilarity of x to an explicitly chosen population of documents [R8];
   7. Whether papers published after t do something similar to x [R26];
   8. Novelty score produced by a language-model judge;
   9. Share of x’s word pairs (bigrams) never seen in the literature before t;
 10. Uptake, as defined in the General Instructions.

  Task A.1                                                                          1.5 pt
  Group the ten measures by the question about the world that each one answers.
  You decide how many groups there are; name each group by a question in plain
  words and justify the grouping. For each measure, say in one sentence what it
  would wrongly reward.


  Task A.2                                                                          0.5 pt
   Use your grouping to explain how two published claims, “distance from prior work
   measures novelty” and “distance from prior work does not predict reviewers’ novelty
   scores”, can both be true.

§M.B Baselines you must compute

On the same data as each test:

   • B-Q: a frozen sentence embedding of the question, with a linear probe for scores
     or cosine for relations;
   • B-ABS: the same on the abstract (setting (a) only; a reference point, not a target);
   • B-TF: TF-IDF on the same text;
   • B-LEN: length alone;
   • B-JUDGE: one language-model judge, both orders (optional, recommended).

§M.E What would count as a success

Each of these is challenging. Any 3, done cleanly, is a strong result.

   1. A quality readout under which good questions cluster: within a topic, a question’s
      nearest neighbours in Φ predict whether it is good, with length and team size
      controlled.
   2. Choosing, among questions built on the same inputs, the one that will do better,
      clearly above a text-only baseline.
   3. A notion of genuine novelty that forecasts uptake beyond what content novelty and
      quality already predict.
   4. A same-question judgement that agrees with careful human labels about as well as
      two careful humans agree with each other.
   5. Features of good questions, stated in words, that keep their direction on years the
      model never saw.
   6. A representation that agrees with experts on generated ideas within a generating
      system.

§M.F Questions to ask yourself

   • Is a one-sentence question enough to judge it? Measure what each unit of text
     carries before you choose an encoder.
   • For each novelty coordinate: which question about the world does it answer (T2)?
     Does any coordinate try to answer two?
   • For each positive result: does it rank systems or ideas? Topics or questions? Writers
     or contents? Recent fields or good work?
   • Do your quality and novelty readouts pull in opposite directions? If so, keep them
     on separate axes and say how an agent should trade them off.
   • When a result looks too good, look first for information leaking across time.
